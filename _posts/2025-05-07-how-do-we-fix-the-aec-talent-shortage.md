Last post, we dug into the numbers. Now, let’s talk about where we go from here. With limited talent entering the industry, we need to take a hard look at how we attract, develop, and retain the people who want to build a career in AEC.

Growing Mid-level Engineers

Lets start by developing and growing the mid-level engineers, we’re starting to see them leave for opportunities that offer better pay and better work-life balance.

We need to acknowledge bad work-life balance habits are into the industry. To keep talent we need to pride them with growth opportunities and it must be growth they want not what you see for them. Fair compensate as to what their peers are earning in this and other industries.

The High School -\> Community College -\> Bachelor's Degree Pipeline

This one’s twofold. First, school is expensive. The community college to university pipeline offers a more affordable way to get a degree, but it’s sometimes looked down on when people are making choices on their future.

Second—we have an awareness problem. Ask a high schooler to name five careers in AEC… you’ll probably get a blank stare. We need to build awareness of AEC in high schools, explaining how this is a stable, fulfilling career, and well paying. We need to also provide scholarships and create clearer paths from community college to university. Lastly, have robust internships for students to keep the engagment and excitement about the industry.

Tapping into Global Talent

Developing international center of excellences. This will help support engineering and architectural work freeing up North American teams.  
We can—and should—also bring in foreign-trained engineers and architects. But we need to recognize international credentials. Additionally, Streamline visa and permanent residency processes.

Licensing Bottlenecks

To become a licensed architect in the U.S., you’re looking at, 5 to 7 years of education, 3,740 hours of documented work experience. Now a six-part, 33-hour exam. I am just tired thinking about all of that. A lot of students aren’t even sure they want to sit for it. (Yes, based an unscientific poll I took at career fairs.) In Canada, it’s a bit lighter, 2,800 hours of work experience and 4 half-day exams.  
And for many retiring architects, licensure was a *lot* less complicated.

For engineers, it’s not much easier. In the U.S., 4 years of experience and an 8-hour PE exam. In Canada, similar work experience—but in some provinces your degree has to be from a recognized school, and you sit for a 2.5-hour ethics/professional practice exam.

How do we Connect with Gen Z and Gen Alpha?

We need to show how AEC work shapes communities, tackles climate challenges, and drives innovation. It’s not just lines and permits—it’s legacy work. We’ve got to make this field exciting again. Show students what they can *become*, not just what the job pays.

Upskilling Current Talent

If you think the pace of change is fast now… just wait.

We’ve already gone from clipboards to tablets to drones in the span of a few years. Now generative AI is speeding things up even more.

We need to invest in learning and development. Make sure our teams stay current even our senior staff. Create space for experimentation and adaptation.

The goal here is to start conversation—about how we grow, how we adapt, and how we future-proof the AEC industry. I’m not saying I have it all figured out, but I do believe that if we start talking now, we can start moving in the right direction.

We already know what’s coming.
