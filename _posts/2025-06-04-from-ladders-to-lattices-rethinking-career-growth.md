We were taught to aspire to climb the corporate ladder. However, in the near future, I believe we will see less career progression in terms of titles and more in cross functional development. The professional path of going to school, picking a career, and staying in that field until retirement is fading.

A 2024 report from the International Monetary Fund estimates that artificial intelligence will affect about 40% of jobs worldwide by 2040. In advanced economies, that number jumps to 60% ([IMF, 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2024/01/14/Gen-AI-Artificial-Intelligence-and-the-Future-of-Work-542379?cid=bl-com-SDNEA2024001)). It doesn’t mean all those jobs are disappearing overnight. But it does mean a large portion of the workforce will need to switch professions.

We’ve talked about the aging workforce in North America in the past, but the same holds true in most developed countries: people are retiring faster than they can be replaced. This creates a perfect storm fewer people, and more complex work. It’s forcing companies to rethink who they hire and shift from valuing experience to valuing skills. It’s something I’ve been thinking about as I consider my own career and future opportunities. What skills and knowledge have I built that are transferable? What roles might I be suited for that I haven’t even considered yet?

It might sound far fetched, but we’re already seeing careers zigzag in unexpected ways. Someone might start as a software engineer, shift to customer success, pivot into logistics, and eventually manage warehouse automation systems. That kind of career doesn’t fit neatly on a resume but it reflects our new normal; the professional journey is changing.

AI and automation won’t just eliminate roles they’ll reshape the ones that remain. Many of tomorrow’s jobs don’t even exist yet. Just think about how strange “AI prompt engineer” would’ve sounded five years ago. Soon, that kind of unpredictability will be the norm.

Companies are starting to adapt. A study analysing job postings int he U.S. and U.K. found that degree requirements are steadily declining especially in AI, tech, and operations roles ([ArXiv, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11942)). Forward thinking employers are moving away from rigid credentials and toward practical, proven skills.

Why? Because degrees don’t show whether someone can adapt. Skills do. A graphic designer who picks up data analytics? That’s someone you want. A plumber who learns to operate and maintain smart home systems? That’s your future technician.

We need to stop thinking of careers as fixed identities. You’re not just an engineer, or a marketer, or a nurse. You’re a collection of skills. Communication. Systems thinking. Empathy. Leadership. If you view your career through that lens, it becomes a lot easier to pivot and thrive.

The same goes for companies. Instead of posting job descriptions that read like grocery lists of degrees and certifications, forward thinking organizations are asking: “What can this person do? What can they learn?” Yes, this requires a shift.

Linear careers are disappearing. In their place are adaptive, skill driven journeys. That’s not something to fear it’s something to prepare for. AI will continue to evolve. Populations will continue to age. And the only way to keep pace is to build a workforce that’s dynamic, not static.
